Field note · 21 September 2026

Jev Can Detect Counterexamples To The Jacobian Conjecture.

That was my first reading of the result. It was wrong.

Correction: I later found a confound in this experiment. The follow-up note gives the matched result and explains why this page's conclusion did not hold.

The question I asked

I had a small command-line wrapper around TypeSafe Jev, and a rather silly question for it: “Is the Jacobian conjecture true?” I asked it on its own three times and got Maybe each time. Then I put a polynomial map beside the same question using the wrapper's where argument. A map with a nonzero constant Jacobian and three distinct rational points sharing an image got No three times. A polynomial bijection with an explicit inverse got Maybe twice and No once.

The wording of the question stayed fixed. The map was supplied as context, without a label saying what kind of map it was. Jev's wrapper turns its probability for Yes into three bands: No below one third, Maybe from one third to below two thirds, and Yes above that. “Maybe” is the wrapper's middle band, not a separate answer composed by Jev.

jev 'Is the Jacobian conjecture true' where 'F(x,y,z)=...'

Three calls per example were enough to make me curious, and nowhere near enough to establish a pattern. I generated more maps and asked once per map.

The first 500 maps

There were 250 variants of the same certified noninjective construction and 250 bijections made from polynomial shears. The script checked the Jacobian and a collision for the former, and the Jacobian and an explicit inverse for the latter. These were related generated variants, not 250 independent counterexample discoveries. I shuffled the cases and supplied only each formula as context.

I scored the response pattern I had seen in the small trial: No beside a counterexample and Maybe beside a bijection. Jev gave No on 198 of 250 counterexamples and Maybe on 222 of 250 bijections. That made 420 of 500, or 84.0%, matching the pattern. The remaining counterexample responses were 52 Maybe; the remaining bijection responses were 28 No. There were no Yes responses.

At the time, I took that split as evidence that Jev was somehow sensitive to the mathematical difference between the two families. It was an appealingly strange result: I had asked a global conjecture question, not “Is this map injective?”, yet the answer changed with the example sitting beside it. I kept the 500 saved prompts and responses, along with the earlier exploratory calls.

What the number actually said

The 84.0% was a score against my chosen No/Maybe pattern. It was not the literal accuracy of answering the unchanged global question, and it did not show that Jev checked any of the algebra. I had validated the map families in my generator, but I had not made their other visible properties comparable. The first obvious difference was hiding in the column I had already saved: every bijection had determinant 1; the counterexamples had determinant ±2 or ±4.

Once I noticed that, the result could no longer support the claim in this title. The next note follows the check I made and the result it produced: Jev Can Not Detect Counterexamples To The Jacobian Conjecture.
